About

I am an Imaging Science PhD student in the Medical AI Lab (MAIL) at Washington University in St. Louis and the Mallinckrodt Institute of Radiology. My work asks a practical question: how can we turn complex medical images into reliable, interpretable measurements of disease?

My current research focuses on genetically mediated autism, where I use MRI and computational morphometry to study whether inherited risk leaves measurable signatures in brain structure and white matter. Rather than building black-box models alone, I am interested in imaging features that help explain what changed, where it changed, and how those changes relate to biology.

My background is in building reusable imaging software. I led development of the fNIRS data framework behind the Optical-imaging XNAT-enabled Informatics (OXI) platform, creating XNAT-based data structures and workflows for optical neuroimaging data organization, processing, and collaboration. I have also developed Docker and XNAT-based workflows for radiomics, neuroimaging, and machine-learning applications. That tool-building perspective shapes my doctoral work: I want methods that are mathematically grounded, reproducible, and usable by researchers beyond my own lab.

Current Focus

Finding Brain Signatures of Genetically Mediated Autism

My doctoral research studies whether computational imaging can identify reproducible brain signatures associated with 16p11.2 deletion and duplication, two genetic variants linked to autism and neurodevelopmental differences.

To do this, I am developing non-mass-preserving transport-based morphometry methods that can measure tissue growth, loss, remodeling, and structural change without assuming that anatomy is simply redistributed. The broader goal is to create interpretable, AI-ready imaging features that can be reused across neurodevelopment, neurodegeneration, cancer, and precision medicine.
